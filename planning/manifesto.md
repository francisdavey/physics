The name of the overaching website or project (as yet to be created) is something like "bumper fun book" (of maths or physics or both).

The underlying goal is for me to understand maths and physics better, by expliaining it in accordance with the manifesto.

First, we try to tell the whole truth. This means not:
- Failing to mention places where our treatment breaks down
- Failing to mention hidden assumptions
- Failing to precisely define things that we talk about
- Use technical ideas that may mislead

My belief is that, frequently, a hidden asumption or place where something fails will reveal deep and interesting mathematics or physics. One goal is to explore those depths as part of any normal account.

An example of failure of precise definition is well expressed by the late W.L. Burke in "Applied Differential Geometry". He rightly complains that many treatments of Lagrangian dynamics talk about integrating 
over a Lagrangian but fail to say **what space** the Lagrangian is a 1-form over. His explanation helped me (and him I suspect) understand something that was very mysterious when the subject was 
explained - how is it that we are differentiating wrt x dot (velocity) as also t. His location of the Lagrangian in relation to the contact bundle helped. This is an example where more formalisation
clarifies. Many accounts of the subject don't really explain that they are assuming that the paths are **parametrised** and that there's a necessary one-form that enforces that x dot is the real 
"velocity" which must pull back to zero on any real path.

Examples of technical ideas that may mislead include:
- "soluble" or "solvable" used in the context of polynomials do not mean "can be solved" which is how the terms are usually used, but have the subtext "by radicals". I see many confused by that.
- References frames in special relativity are quite complicated objects. In particular they are usually assumed to cover the whole of spacetime. But often language like "X 'sees'" are used to mean
 "X has set up a reference frame, in which they measure...". This causes confusion in situations like the twin paradox.

 Second, everything must be motivated. We do not define a mathematical object until we need it and motivate its use before introducing it. A great many mathematical texts start with a wall of
 definitions.
