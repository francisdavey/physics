[1] The idea of naive integration given by Bartels, is disappointing because it invokes dt, which is something we wanted to define in the first place.
[2] It is possible to give a "pure" cogerm based definition, which I believe involves scaling the parameter of the germs handed to the cogerm in proportion to the size of the steps involved in summation. The end result is the same but avoids mention of dt.
[3] This investigation considers whether another approach might be (a) more natural (b) more in line with Leibniz (c) allow more flexible integration and further lines of inquiry. Albeit more complicated and sensible to postpone after introducing cogerms.
[4] If we define a variable from being a map from (curve, point on curve) to the reals and instead make the domain (a sequences of points, a point) to the reals, that comes closer to the Leibniz idea. What we are doing is effectively postponing the work of taking the limit.
[4A] The idea is that a curve is approximated by a sequence of points on it (the vertices of Leibniz's "polygon"). Let us call "polygon" a sequence of points and a "polygon series for a curve" a series of polygons with edge lengths (h) that converges to zero and that converge to the curve. In his thinking the points are infinitesimally close together, so we simulate that by having a sereis of increasingly good approximations to the curve.
[4B] Variables like $x$ just return the x-value of the point. They don't care about adjacent points in the polygon.
[4C] dv is the variable that, on any sequence (x_i) returns v(x_(i+1))-v(x_(i)).
[5] In this language equality applied to variables (which includes of course differentials) will be dependent on some number $k$. There are two ways to approach this:
[5A} We assign an order to an expression. Variables which are functions on the manifold have order zero. d increases order as does multiplication. We impose a constraint that only terms of the same order may be added. Then we say $A=0$ at a point if for all epsilon > 0, there is a sequence of sequences of points, (S_i=x_ij....) such that tehre is an N>0 for which all i>N, abs(A(x_ij)) < epsilon h_ij ^ k, where h_i=x_ij - xi(j-1). i.e. the length of the ith polgyonal approximation's edge.
[5B] We could not impose that order discipline and have a kth equality relation =_k with the same definition.
[5C] I've not said what the "point" is. I am not sure what the right approach is. The idea is that the sequence of points approximates a curve and that if we have a sequence of sequences S_i that will "converge" to the curve, we don't necessarily want to require the point of interest to be one of the points on any sequence. I suggest a point is just a point in the underlying manifold such that there a point in the S_i Converges to it, i.e for all epsilon > 0 there is an N such that i>n means there is a poitn in S_i closer to p than epsilon.
[6]  There is a slight modification to the above for higher orders. Here we have to be able to express the progression of variables. here $A=0 (d^2 v=0)$ means A=0 for S_i such that the condition $d^2=0$ holds. I.e. we restrict to a specific progression of variables.
[7] If we treat integration as supplying a series of polygons approximating the curve of integration to the integrand. we can I think do a Henstock style integral and a Stieltjies style integral. 
[8] I think that a Henstock style would not work for higher order integrands but that is expected. It seems to me that the rigidy of a progression of variables constraint can't exist under Henstock (the nlab page suggests that ddx vanishes in such a situation).
[9] But we don't need to treat integrals just like that. We can treat integrals just as sums over an S_i to be supplied. I.e. they work exactly in this framework. Equality sorts out when we do the actual convergence. Integral reduces order by 1. 
[10] Leibniz thought of differentials as "infinitessimals" in a sense close to an infinite sequence of numbers at each point (tending to the relevant value), much as in Hardy/"chap infinitessimals". This seems more his style of approach.
[11] I think that allows all sorts of very natural use of notation and work.
